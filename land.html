The Land

A wooded hillside tract of roughly seven acres in Miami Township, Hamilton County, Ohio.

Bedrock

The hill is built of the Kope Formation: gray, limy shale interbedded with thin layers of limestone, laid down on the floor of a shallow sea in the Late Ordovician, roughly 450 million years ago. The Kope belongs to the Cincinnatian rocks that made this region a classic ground for the study of Ordovician life, and it is rich in fossils, including brachiopods, bryozoans, crinoids, trilobites, and mollusks. One of its subunits, the Wesselman Tongue, takes its name from a type section in Miami Township.

History

The tract lies within the Miami Purchase, the land between the Great and Little Miami Rivers that John Cleves Symmes acquired from the Continental Congress in 1788. Symmes founded nearby North Bend, the third settlement in the Purchase, and on January 2, 1790, Hamilton County was organized as the second county of the Northwest Territory. Today the hillside is forest, and it is held to remain so.

Forest

A mature canopy covers the hillside, and two creeks meet at the low point of the tract. The forest is left to grow as it will.

Rare plants

Three plants of conservation concern grow on the property. None is a naturally occurring population on this site. Locations and numbers are not published.

Florida torreya (Torreya taxifolia)

A federally endangered conifer native to a small area along the Apalachicola River in the Florida Panhandle and southwestern Georgia. Mature trees grow on the property, far north of that range.

American chestnut (Castanea dentata)

Pure American chestnut, not a hybrid. Once among the largest trees of eastern North American forests, it was nearly eliminated by chestnut blight, an introduced fungus identified in New York in 1904. In Ohio, its historic range lay mainly in the eastern half of the state.

Running buffalo clover (Trifolium stoloniferum)

A native clover that spreads by runners along the ground. Long thought extinct, it was found again in Ohio in 1985. It was removed from the federal endangered species list in 2021 on the basis of recovery and is listed by the State of Ohio as potentially threatened. A few wild populations persist in southwestern Ohio, including in Hamilton County.
